1. The answer to your question

Yes, people day-trade MNQ profitably. No, nobody with a verified record makes $1,000 a day on $30,000, and the reason is arithmetic rather than skill. The best documented intraday futures records pay $15–$125 a day on a $30,000 account. The single closest twin to you I could find anywhere — Pau Perdices, a publicly-tracked, commission-net, discretionary intraday micro-gold + micro-Nasdaq account — made $7,163 net on $10,000 in 8.3 months through a −21.2% drawdown: about $124/day scaled to $30k. The top 500 out of ~450,000 Taiwanese day traders, measured out of sample the following year and net of all fees, earned +37.9 basis points a day = $114/day on $30k. That is the 99.9th percentile of the largest audited day-trading dataset in existence.

$1,000/day net on MNQ is 500 index points a day, every day. MNQ's average whole-session range is 387 points and its average first-90-minutes range is 267 points — so one lot is arithmetically impossible, it is 129% of the entire daily range. The honest grid is required gross edge per round trip = 500/(lots × round trips) + 1.25 points. At 10 lots and 6 round trips a day that needs 9.58 points of gross edge per round trip. The measured gross edge available on MNQ is 1.05–1.50 points — our own five-method result, independently confirmed from outside on the exact contract by Mesfin (2026), who tested 14 signal families on 947 MNQ sessions and found a ceiling of 0.07–1.50 points. Every cell of that grid exceeds the ceiling, and the friction term alone is 1.25, so no amount of size or frequency can push the requirement below it.

And here is the reconciliation with the forums, which I think is the single most useful thing in this report: the posts are probably true about the dollars and false about the capital. Topstep's own published 2025 statistics say 51.8% of persistent participants pass an evaluation at least once — so the posters are real — but only 0.71% ever reach a live funded account. A man "making $1,000 a day" is nearly always doing it on a ~$150,000 prop account, which is 0.67% a day. You are asking for 3.33% a day. Same edge, five times the capital, and the capital is not his.

What is genuinely on the table. Our own gross edge, at a sane size, independently produces $15–$30/day — which lands within a dollar of the broker-verified intraday index records. Push to 10 lots with a 4R average winner and the arithmetic permits $400–500/day, at the price of a ten-loss streak costing 17–27% of the account. So $1,000/day on $30k is not impossible in arithmetic — it is impossible in drawdown. Capital required to run it survivably: $300k–$900k, centred around $400k.

The three cross-lens resolutions no single source could see

(a) Your 90-minute habit is vindicated by the instrument — and the same measurement reverses the recommendation

Al Brooks asserts the day's high or low occurs within the first 90 minutes about 90% of the time. That claim is the load-bearing justification for trading only the open, and its one public check (TradingView, n=29 ES days) got 59%, not 90%. We measured it on our own MNQ tape and Brooks is right: 90.0% — the public refutation failed on a definition, measuring whether the range survived rather than whether an extreme occurred. But the same measurement then reverses the advice:

So the window is structurally continuation geometry — price has just left one extreme and is travelling toward another that prints in the afternoon — and stopping at 11:00 ET abandons an unfinished range nine days in ten. Worse: your window opens on the single most expensive five minutes of the MNQ day. 09:30–09:35 ET has a median 3-tick spread, $1.36 a crossing against $0.86 at 10:15, and 3 contracts a side instead of 5 — widest bars, widest spread and thinnest book are all the same five minutes. Meanwhile MNQ's spread is tightest 16:00–19:00Z, which is the part of the day nobody looks at.

(b) Four literatures 34 years apart are measuring one latent variable: "is today busy?"

Zarattini's opening-range Relative Volume (2024, dose-response −0.02R / +0.08R / +0.38R) is Toby Crabel's "Early Entry 2" from 1990 — first-bar volume against the same bar over the previous 15–20 days. It is also Dalton's Open Drive (17% of sessions, 62.5% continuation, the only opening type in Market Profile that survives a 2:1 test) and the ORB width tiers (wide opening ranges >0.6×ATR continue 74.2% on NQ against 63.6% for narrow ones). Four traditions, one measurement. Use RelVol: it is the only version that is objective and has a published dose-response. Corollary worth saying out loud: the Market Profile open-type taxonomy is probably not a separate research programme at all, just the same measurement with a subjective classifier bolted on.

(c) The MGC contradiction resolves into a decision

The order-flow lens concluded gold is the friendly instrument — a 30-tick MGC stop carries only 15% friction, and it is the only configuration where $1,000/day looked arithmetically reachable. The instrument lens, having actually walked the book, found MGC's practical ceiling is ~5 lots at $12–13/lot all-in. The instrument lens wins because it measured rather than assumed the constant scales. The synthesis nobody states: MGC is the friendlier instrument PER LOT and the worse instrument AT SIZE. So gold is where to test tight-stop, level-anchored methods at 1–3 lots, and MNQ is the only instrument that can ever carry income size.

★ A consequence that reprices a lot of existing work: the paper engine's fabricated 0.1% adverse fill is $29.39/lot on MNQ against a measured real 10-lot book-walk of $0.35/lot — 84× punitive — but on MGC it is $4.37 against $4.60 measured, i.e. roughly realistic. MGC's paper P&L is far more trustworthy than MNQ's, and any correction must be per-instrument. A single global fudge factor would be exactly the "right number beside a wrong one" bug.

The theorem that reclassifies our biggest null. Kaminski & Lo (J. And a warning about decay, from the best-documented anomaly in our own contract family

The overnight drift was a 22-year, t=7.1, bootstrap-verified, mechanism-identified, RFS-published effect: the 02:00–03:00 ET hour returned 3.7%/yr and was more than 60% of the ES contract's entire close-to-close return, causally pinned by a daylight-saving natural experiment. Ten weeks ago, on 1 July 2026, the original authors published "The Disappearing Overnight Drift": that window has averaged close to zero since 2021, "the same pattern holds in the E-mini Nasdaq-100 (NQ)", and the channel that died was end-of-day order-imbalance dispersion, halving from 6.5% to 2.9% because algorithmic liquidity providers slice flow more finely. Two ETFs launched to harvest it closed after fourteen months.

★ Two consequences. (a) Ship a decay monitor with every edge: name the mechanism, name the observable channels that drive it, and monitor those — P&L is far too noisy to detect decay in time. (b) The specific thing that died is order-flow imbalance dispersion, which is exactly the quantity our 250ms depth capture is mining. The Fed has just documented it being compressed out of the market by better execution algorithms. Related: the best practitioner strategy in the sweep has been independently replicated and independently found to have decayed — Paz (2026) reproduced the Noise Area in-sample at Sharpe 1.34 against the published 1.33, then got 0.39 out of sample over May 2024–Mar 2026, and Zarattini's own live tracking table agrees (2025 YTD +1.0%, five of six months negative).
