§2Where the money was — the MNQ run census

Over the 30 archive days in the tape (28 full sessions plus two half-days), MNQ printed 1,338 big runs — a directional shove of at least 40 points that completed inside 30 minutes. How a "run" is defined — and how we checked if we caught it

I ran this on 1-minute MNQ closes, day by day, using the same gate code the live desk runs (gazbot7.deciders). A run starts at any bar, scans forward up to 30 minutes tracking the directional extreme, and ends the first time price gives back more than half of the best move it had made. If that best move reached 40 points or more, it counts as a big run. The scanner then jumps past the extreme so runs never overlap and nothing gets double-counted. To score participation, I replayed the two momentum gates — gate_grind (slope floor 0.4, fast slope) and gate_thrust (thrust 1.5, amplitude floor 0.0004) — bar by bar, and asked one question: did either gate open a position in the run's direction at any point between the run's start and its peak? If yes, we showed up.

Across two weeks of V5 and the first two days of the V7 board, the desk tells the same story twice: the money is made when we push a move that is already going, and the money is bled when we lean against a move to fade it. Two-sided — and why the short tilt this fortnight is a trap to read wrong

This is the part that is easy to misread, so I want to be careful. On the V7 board the short side made +$2,884 over 155 trades at 54%, while the long side lost -$1,055 over 259 trades at 30%. It is tempting to conclude "go short, stop going long." That conclusion is wrong, and the data itself says why.

Look at grind_fast, the best strategy on the board. It made money on both sides — +$748 long and +$552 short. A genuinely two-sided momentum strategy was green in both directions over the same two days. Meanwhile the strategies that were forced long-only — the rg_long family — lost -$1,092 across 117 trades at 26%. So the long book did not bleed because "long is bad." It bled because the fortnight was a down tape, and the strategies that could only buy had no way to take the paying side, while the two-sided strategies simply followed the move down and got paid.

The edge is momentum, not direction. Killing the fade book is not the same as going long-only. This fortnight momentum happened to pay more on the short side because the tape trended down; a two-sided thrust strategy captured that automatically, and a forced-long one could not. If we respond to a bearish fortnight by biasing the desk long, we would have been long into exactly the move that paid the shorts. The correct response to the short tilt is to keep both sides armed and let the move pick the direction.

The census work backs this up from the other end: over 1,338 big MNQ runs the desk showed up for 77% of them, 92% of the 120-point-plus moves, and 100% of the top fifteen. We are not missing the moves — participation is not the problem. The leak is between the moves, in the trades we take when nothing is thrusting, which is precisely where the fade book lives and precisely where it bleeds.

The amplitude floor, and what to actually do

One refinement earns its place rather than just meaning "trade less." In V7 the thrust trades taken when ATR was above 0.07% of price made +$1,322 over 36 trades, while the thrust trades in the quieter 0.04–0.07% band netted essentially flat at +$24 over 76 trades. Thrust wants amplitude. A thin tape produces thrust signals that do not carry, and the honest read is to stand down on the small ones rather than dress them up. This is in-sample on two days, so I flag it as a lean, not a law — but it agrees with the V5 amplitude-floor work and it costs nothing to respect.

The prescription is not a new gate. It is a reallocation. Feed the thrust book — grind_fast, thrust_cont, thrust_loose — capital and both-sided arming, with the amplitude floor keeping it off thin tape. Starve the fade book — passive_chop, capit_loose, the coil-break fades — because two independent windows of honest fills say it is a structural drain, not a run of bad luck. And do not let a bearish fortnight talk the desk into a long-only bias: the winner was two-sided, and that is the whole point.

KEEP & FEED the confirmed-thrust book, two-sided, amplitude-floored. KILL the passive-chop and capitulation fades. WATCH the V7 short tilt as regime, not signal — re-check when the tape turns.

What to actually do — gate the long side, don't kill it

The temptation is to switch off longs entirely, and the raw numbers almost justify it. I'd stop short of that, for two honest reasons. The right shape of this desk, on this evidence, is an unconstrained short book plus a tightly-gated, trend-confirmed long book (grind- and thrust_cont-style), and nothing that buys dips blind.

KILL the blind long book the long-only reversion sims (rg_long_*, capit_*) that lost $1,563 firing counter-trend. KEEP & ARM the circuit breaker to condition-gate longs — currently dormant at zero trades, the #1 lever. WATCH the short book run — it's the engine, don't touch it.

Same lesson from two directions: the answer is not "pick a tighter number," it's "stop picking one number at all."

The prescription — a vol-adaptive chandelier. Set the trail distance as a function of live ATR, not a fixed point/percent width. Tighten hard in the low-vol bucket (a quiet winner that peaks at $50 shouldn't need $15 of room — that's clipping runners for nothing) and give a fast-tape winner proportionally more slack so a normal high-ATR swing doesn't knock it out at the first wobble. The give-back fraction is already a stable third; the goal is to make that third the deliberate third rather than an accident of whichever flat width was hard-coded. The census backs the upside: we show up for 77% of big MNQ runs and 100% of the top-15, so the runners exist to be captured — the leak is purely in how we ride them out.

The exit spine is the desk's real product this week, and it's working: the trailing chandelier books green on the live desk when nothing else does, and on the shadow board it and the fixed target between them carry roughly $10k of profit against the stop pile. The one honest leak is the give-back, and it is not a mystery — it's a flat third of peak that turns into real dollars precisely when the tape is fast. A flat trail can't win that fight in both regimes at once.

KEEP the chandelier — it is the profit centre, live and shadow. WATCH the flat-width A/B — tighter won this week but on 9-11 trades in one chop regime; don't promote a single number off it. §6The order book: our honest look at whether the wall tells us anything

For weeks the standing hope has been that the ten levels of resting liquidity on either side of the market would tell us where price is about to go — that a fat wall of bids means "buy", a fat wall of offers means "sell it's about to get rejected". We finally have enough book to answer it properly, and the answer is the disciplined one: on our home contract the book tells us almost nothing, on the other two it whispers something real but smaller than a single tick, and every folk-theory about "fading the wall" comes out backwards. This is a keep-capturing, don't-trade-on-it chapter.

Here is what we are actually sitting on. The two-week depth store (depth.db, ten levels deep, a fresh snapshot every 250 milliseconds) holds 1,812,648 book snapshots on MNQ, 1,142,749 on MES and 1,073,720 on MGC across July 9–17. So this is not a thin-n complaint about the book itself. There is plenty of book. The problem is what's in it.

Test two: "fade the wall" is backwards.

The seductive theory is contrarian: a giant offer overhead is a ceiling, so sell into it. The data says the opposite. The last hope was absorption — price pushes up into a big resting offer, the offer soaks it up and holds, and the theory says that stalled push snaps back. I built the event directly: a recent up-push running into a wall three times the median size, then measured whether price continued through or reversed. Across 7,373 such events on MNQ it continued 47.2% of the time and reversed 52.8%; MES 42.7% / 57.3% across 4,310; MGC 47.2% / 52.8% across 6,715. So there is a faint reversal lean — but 53–57% at the five-second horizon is exactly what plain mean-reversion gives you after any sharp push, wall or no wall. The wall adds nothing you couldn't get from the price move alone, and the edge sits under the spread again. "Absorption reverses" is, at best, a coin flip wearing a lab coat.

The three folk-theories, scored: imbalance predicts direction — true but a quarter of a tick, buried under a full-tick spread. Fade the wall — refuted, the book is momentum-aligned, and fading the MES offer wall loses 70% of the time. Absorption reverses — a coin flip (47–53%) indistinguishable from ordinary post-push reversion. None of the three clears the cost of trading it.

Why MNQ — the one we actually trade — is the deafest of the three

This is the awkward part. The book is least informative exactly where we live. MNQ's +0.0024 is the weakest of the three; MES and MGC, which we barely touch, carry what little signal exists. That fits what we already know about MNQ: it's fast, thin at each level, and the resting size churns so quickly that a "wall" is gone before you can react to it. The e-mini (MES) is deeper and slower, so its book holds its shape long enough to mean something — but "something" here is two hundredths of a correlation and a tenth of a tick. It is a tilt, not a trigger.

What the book is actually good for

Not as an entry — on no contract, in no configuration, does the book clear the spread as a standalone reason to fire. That door is closed and I'd stop knocking on it. Where it has a plausible future is as a small exit or veto tilt on MES only: when we're already long and the offer stack ahead is heavy and holding, that 70% continuation read is at least pointing the right way, and it costs nothing to let a runner breathe a beat longer. That's a nudge on a position we already hold, not a new bet — which is the only place a quarter-tick signal can pay, because we're not crossing the spread to use it.

The other honest note: the V7 book is three days old. All the numbers above lean on the two-week depth.db history; the 35-million-row V7 capture has barely started, and it only covers MNQ so far — the one contract where the book is deafest. Before we build anything, even the MES exit-tilt, we need the V7 book and tape running together across MES and MGC for a couple of weeks so we're testing the world the live desk actually trades in, not last fortnight's.

Verdict. No standalone entry on any contract — the strongest book lean moves price ~0.06–0.12 tick against a ≥1-tick spread. "Fade the wall" refuted — the book is momentum-aligned; fading the MES offer wall lost 70% of the time. Keep as an MES exit/veto tilt only, and keep capturing — extend V7 book+tape to MES/MGC before we trust anything built on it. The book is real, it's just quieter than the spread.
