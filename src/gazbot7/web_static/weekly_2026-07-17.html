§2MNQ ran a thousand times this month — and we banked none of it. Presence was never the edge; capture is.

Re-run the whole month of MNQ tape and the same picture comes back every time: this contract moves. Somewhere around a thousand clean 40-point-plus runs inside half an hour, roughly a hundred and fifty grand of directional travel laid out on the table, monsters up to 700-plus points. The census: the runs are absolutely there

I re-derived this from the archive tape directly, one-minute MNQ bars, June 14 through July 17, asking a simple question of every bar: within the next thirty minutes, does price travel 40 points or more in one direction? Counting distinct, non-overlapping runs, the month printed 969 of them. Count every overlapping window the way a signal gate actually sees the tape and you clear 1,300 — that is where the ~1,338 figure comes from, and both are true; they are just two ways of counting the same abundance. Presence is free. A backtest gate signals inside most of these runs — that is trivially easy and it is worth nothing. Signalling is not catching. To bank a run you have to enter well, hold it while it pays, and get out before it hands the money back. We do the first and fail the other two.

The desk has been net-green for a fortnight. That is true, and it is also the trap. Green is not coming from a balanced pair of edges — it is one strong edge dragging a dead weight. Retiring reversion means "stop selling rips you are betting will reverse." It does not mean "stop selling." When a genuine downward thrust fires, momentum shorts it and that is exactly right. Freeze the shorts and you throw away half of the only book that pays. The rule is about the setup, not the direction.

What the A/B actually says about trail width

The board ran three flat chandelier widths this week, and the raw scores lean tighter-is-better: k20 at +$18.5, k25 at -$36.5, k35 at -$101. What it does confirm is the mechanism: the widest arm (k35) is the one that gave back positive dollars on average and scored worst, i.e. a wide flat trail sits too far back and bleeds. That's consistent with the 25-day archive replay, where a fixed 3.5-ATR trail was beaten by an ATR-conditional graded trail by +$245 on the thrust family and +$1,653 on grind. Same lesson from two directions: the answer is not "pick a tighter number," it's "stop picking one number at all."

The prescription — a vol-adaptive chandelier. Set the trail distance as a function of live ATR, not a fixed point/percent width. Tighten hard in the low-vol bucket (a quiet winner that peaks at $50 shouldn't need $15 of room — that's clipping runners for nothing) and give a fast-tape winner proportionally more slack so a normal high-ATR swing doesn't knock it out at the first wobble. The give-back fraction is already a stable third; the goal is to make that third the deliberate third rather than an accident of whichever flat width was hard-coded. The runs are there to ride — MNQ made a thousand big moves this month, so the runners are never in short supply; the leak is purely in how we ride the ones we're in out to the end.

The verdict

The exit spine is the desk's real product this week, and it's working: the trailing chandelier books green on the live desk when nothing else does, and on the shadow board it and the fixed target between them carry roughly $10k of profit against the stop pile. The one honest leak is the give-back, and it is not a mystery — it's a flat third of peak that turns into real dollars precisely when the tape is fast. A flat trail can't win that fight in both regimes at once.

KEEP the chandelier — it is the profit centre, live and shadow. WATCH the flat-width A/B — tighter won this week but on 9-11 trades in one chop regime; don't promote a single number off it. BUILD the vol-adaptive trail — the give-back scaling with ATR ($15→$23→$37 across the buckets, same 30-34% every time) plus the archive replay's +$245/+$1,653 edge for a graded trail is the strongest, most repeatable exit finding on the board. Make the trail a function of ATR, not a constant.

§6The order book leans the way price is already going — so "fade the wall" is dead, and the only faint pulse is on MES

This is our honest look at the Level-2 order book — the ten-deep ladder of resting bids and offers we've been quietly recording at 250ms on MNQ, MES and MGC. The desk has always wondered whether a big wall of sellers is something to fade, or a stack of bids is somewhere price will bounce. Nine days of tape gives a flat answer: the book barely predicts anything, and the little it does predict points the same way price is already moving — not against it. There is no wall to fade.

The recording is real and deep. Since 09 July we've captured about 1.81 million ten-level snapshots on MNQ, 1.14 million on MES and 1.07 million on MGC — a snapshot every quarter-second, both sides, ten prices deep.
