M2 Could our own gates have caught them?

Short answer, Garrath: no — not in any way worth acting on. I took the 50-odd runs we sat out this week (about $10,900 of hindsight money on the table) and fired all six of our live gates at them mechanically, in the run's direction, in the 10 minutes before each one lit up — then priced the exits honestly on this week's own ticks. Between the six of them they'd have fired on just 19 of the 50 runs and banked about +$419. That's roughly 4% of the money that got away, spread across single-digit trades per gate, and it's the generous number — I fired them ungated, so the live ER and ATR floors would knock it down further. Two of the six gates fired on zero of the 50. The runs we miss, we miss by design.

Why this is a structural miss, not a tuning gap

Look at what the six gates are: four of them (rgv_long, rgv_short, capitulation_long, exhaustion_short) are faders — they exist to sell strength and buy weakness. A fresh run is the exact thing they're built to lean against, so of course they don't catch it; when they do fire it's late (price already stretched) and they're fading the move that's still going. The other two are momentum gates that need a shape the runs don't offer at ignition: grind wants a trend that's already established, thrust wants five bars of confirmed burst. Our runs ignite in one or two bars from a flat or opposite backdrop. No threshold tweak fixes a mechanism aimed the wrong way — you'd need a new gate that triggers on the ignition itself (the vacuum snap, the 1-2 bar impulse), which is the greenfield question, not this one.

M3 The greenfield lab: building a gate to catch the runs

Garrath — this week the tape handed us 71 big MNQ runs (a 15-min move ≥ 75pt) and we sat out 49 of them, ~$10,584 of hindsight 1-lot ceiling left on the table. So I ignored every gate the desk owns, went back to a blank sheet, and tried to invent a brand-new entry that would have shown up for those runs. The honest headline: nothing survived — this movement ends in a NULL, and it's the most useful thing I found all week. Here's the shape of it. The runs are real money: if you could enter at the exact start with the right side, a plain chandelier bags +$4,388 at 96% win, because these runs barely go against you once they leave (avg max-favourable +109pt, avg max-adverse just −10pt). The exit is fine. The money is fine. The entire problem is the entry — and every causal tell I could build off the tape (flow, volume, breakout) turns out to be a coin-flip. The one signal that actually sees almost every run — a 5-minute breakout — sees 48 of the 49 and then bleeds −$5,056 trading them at 49.9% win, because the tape print that starts a real run is statistically identical to the thousand chop-bursts that start nothing. I built four ideas, dug four graves, and there is no gate to arm. Below is the whole autopsy.

Step 1 — who is even catchable? Split the 49 sat-out runs into two families

Before building anything I split the sat-out runs by whether the aggressor tape in the 60s before the run leaned the way the run then went. Family A (flow already leaning the run's way) is the only family with a plausible pre-run tell. Family B fires against the standing flow — a vacuum snap-back — and by construction can't be predicted from flow. Family B is nearly half the book.

So even in the best case the ceiling for any flow-based idea is the 21 Family-A runs — under half the pot. That's the first cold splash.

Step 2 — are the runs even worth chasing? The oracle says yes (and proves the problem is entry, not exit)

I ran an oracle: enter at the exact census-defined start of every sat-out run, on the correct side, and let a normal chandelier work (30pt stop / 25pt arm / 20pt give-back, tick-honest, −$5 round trip). This is cheating on timing — but it isolates whether the runs are tradeable or whether they whip you out. Result: they're beautifully tradeable. That is the whole tension of this movement — the money is sitting right there, and the exit is not the bottleneck.

GRAVE #1 — Flow-continuation (the main hope)

The obvious Family-A idea: fire when volume expands (≥1.5× baseline) AND net aggressor flow leans ≥150 AND price is already ticking that way. Enter with the flow, chandelier exit. Result: 260 trades, −$1,244, −$4.8/trade, 51.5% win — a coin-flip that pays a fee. It saw only 4 of 49 runs. Cause of death: "flow leaning" is a property of almost every minute of the session, not of run-starts. It doesn't separate the 21 catchable runs from the chop.

GRAVE #2 — Naive volume breakout

Strip out flow entirely: fire on a volume spike (≥1.5× baseline) plus any ≥10pt push, go with the push. The textbook "something's happening, get in." Result: 254 trades, −$1,500, −$5.9/trade, 50.0% win. Dead-even hit rate, so the −$5 cost is the whole P&L. Cause of death: a volume spike marks activity, not direction — half the spikes are the top/bottom of a fade.

GRAVE #3 — Chase the flow

Maybe raw aggressor flow is the tell: whenever 60s net flow is extreme (≥300), enter in its direction. Result: 8,589 fires, 451 trades, −$3,444, −$7.6/trade, 48.3% win — the worst of the lot. Cause of death: on this tape strong flow is mildly CONTRARIAN, not continuation — heavy lifting into a level is where price turns. Chasing it is systematically wrong.

GRAVE #4 — Vacuum-fade (try to trade Family B)

Family B fires against the flow, so I tried to trade the snap-back directly: when flow is heavy one way but price is already going the other, bet on the vacuum and enter with price. Result: 61 trades, −$532, −$8.7/trade, 47.5% win. Cause of death: the snap-backs that do run are indistinguishable beforehand from the far more common ones that just bounce 15pt and die. Family B stays declared structurally unpredictable — the data earned that label.

Step 3 — the one that nearly had me: the 5-minute breakout that SEES the runs

Here's the seductive one. A plain Donchian breakout — close takes out the prior 5-minute high (go long) or low (go short) — as a raw signal sees 48 of the 49 sat-out runs. It is genuinely watching the right thing. Then you trade it and it falls apart, because between the 48 real runs it also breaks out into a thousand chop-traps. Mechanical spec exactly as tested:

Caveats stated plainly: this is one week, in-sample, thin per-family n (21 catchable runs), MNQ-only, paper. But the failure is so uniform — every idea lands on 48–52% win — that more data won't rescue it. The problem is structural, not sample size.

The verdict

NULL. Nothing to arm — not even to shadow as a capital gate. The durable, bankable lesson from this movement: the runs are worth ~$4.4k/week only with hindsight timing (their −10pt adverse excursion is itself an artefact of anchoring on the exact extreme). In real time, a run-start is statistically indistinguishable from the chop-bursts around it — the same breakout that catches 48/49 runs is a 50/50 coin-flip that bleeds after cost. Flow is not a continuation tell here (if anything it's contrarian), and the vacuum family (23 of 49) fires against the tape and cannot be predicted. Do not build a "catch the runs" gate off price/volume/flow alone. If there's a next attempt, it is NOT another entry threshold — it's the L2 order-book depletion angle (does the far side thin out before the run?), which this movement did not test and is the only stone left unturned.
