UNCLASS is the biggest bucket, and until this weekend it had never been hunted at all. Not because anybody decided to skip it — because our own classifier could not name it. UNCLASS is the fall-through branch of a four-branch waterfall: if the flow test finds nothing, and the amplitude test finds nothing, and the clock is not the US open, the run lands in UNCLASS. Half the money on this page sat in a bucket whose only definition is “we ran our two instruments over it and neither one lit up.” This weekend is the first time anyone has taken a run at it, and §4 has the result.

Now the part that changes what the money is worth

The $9,610 is a hindsight number. It measures each run close-to-close, from a starting point you can only identify after the move has happened. Nothing that enters after a move has proven itself can collect it. So the first honest job was to price what a run-catcher could reach even if it cheated on the exit.

Thirteen per cent of the runs are finished before any honest trigger can see them. By the time even the fastest detector fires, there is nothing left. Those runs are not catchable by anything that boards; only by something that predicts — and predicting run starts is already a banked null on 22.6 million ticks.

What a realistic capture rate is worth per week

Today: zero. Not “small”. Zero. The rider that participated in 32 of the 62 sat-out runs — half of them — lost $245 on the census week and measured +$14 across 1,289 trades on 236 untouched sessions. That is the honest weekly number and I am not going to dress it up.

The ceiling worth building toward is about $2,000 a week, not $9,610. A deployable detector reaches $1,910–$2,286 of oracle. If a future selector converted a genuinely good third of that — which nothing on this page has yet done — you would be looking at roughly $600–$750 a week, before the round trip. Every conversation about this money should start from those numbers, not from the census headline.

2 THE VERDICT, UP FRONT

No. We did not find a way to ride them. Not one candidate out of roughly forty, across seven separate hunts, survives to a live promotion — and only two survive to shadow. But the week is not a null, because it answered why, and the answer is precise and it is not what we assumed. Detection is solved. A dumb five-minute boarding trigger gets inside 62 of the 62 runs we sat out, at a median three minutes late, with a median 41 points and 63% of the move still in front of it. We can see them. We can get on them. What kills us is selection: that same trigger fires about 34 times a day, roughly one fire in twenty becomes a run, and the rider is risking a 33–48 point stop to collect a median 12–41 points. The arithmetic never closes. Every wide-exit variant, every causal filter, every cluster-specific gate and every book read we tried is an attempt to fix that ratio, and none of them did — the ATR band that looked like the answer turns out to concentrate the same money into a quarter of the trades rather than find any, worth +$368 in total on the bottom line. The one instrument that would break it — something that says at the board moment “given this volatility, is this a 3-ATR move or a 12-ATR one?” — does not exist on this desk, and every board-time read we own correlates with a run’s eventual size in ATR units at r ≤ 0.13. That is a missing instrument, not a failed strategy, and building it is the next attempt. Two things do go forward to shadow, both of them instrumented questions rather than money-makers, and one live arm gets retired: rider_w5, shipped off last week’s version of this same work at a quoted +$19.29 a trade, has taken 51 forward trades since 08-17 for −$1,471, or −$28.84 a trade, and nobody had graded it until now.

3 THE RIDER — the pooled, direction-agnostic run-catcher

The instruction was to stop trying to predict a run and instead board it after it has proven itself, then measure what is left. One rule, both directions, all clusters, all sessions — a pooled run-catcher that does not care why the tape is moving. This is the piece that matters most, so it gets the full working: the spec, how late we board and what remains, the exit swept wider than this desk has ever run one, the causal filter and its placebos, the test that killed it, and the router rule that would keep the survivor dark until conditions are ripe.

The mechanical spec — RIDER_ALL / board_atrband

Sampled at each completed one-minute bar. Everything here is causal — known before the trade.

ATR20      = mean 1-min (high − low) over the last 20 minutes
             ★ this is the exact number the live router already prints, so it is deployable as written

FIRE  when  11 ≤ ATR20 < 16                       (a BAND — both edges carry weight)
      AND   |net move over 10 min| ≥ 2.5 × ATR20   (the move has proven itself)
      AND   ER(10 min) ≥ 0.50                      (it got there in a straight line)

DIRECTION  = sign of the 10-minute move            (no view, no prediction, no classification)
STOP       = 3.0 × ATR20
TARGET     = 6.0 × ATR20
TIME CAP   = flat at 240 minutes
LOCKOUT    = 60 minutes between fires
COSTS      = $2.00/point, $1.50 per round trip

“What was left” is measured to the run’s own terminus — the furthest the move got before giving back a full ATR — with no costs and no stop. It is a pure oracle: the most any rider could have taken, not what one would take.

A five-minute confirmation catches every one of the 62 runs we sat out, and catches them fast: three minutes in at the median, ten minutes at the very worst. At that moment the median run still has 41 points and 63% of its move in front of it. Detection latency, on this trigger, is simply not the problem.

That directly contradicts the prior this desk has been carrying. The detection-latency finding we have quoted all month — median 119 points already moved, 16 points left, 12% capture — was measured on a 30-minute window with net ≥ 80 and ER ≥ 0.35. That is a much heavier confirmation and it buys a much later seat. Shortening the window from 30 minutes to 5 moves the median from “16 points left” to “41 points left”. The latency was a property of the instrument we were measuring with, not of the tape.

And the thing that table does not show, which is the whole problem: the same W=5 / k=1.0 trigger fires 34 times a day. Over the 35-day lake it produces 1,180 boards, of which about 60 a month are these runs. The other 96% are ordinary tape.

On the surface the wide-exit thesis is confirmed. The live-desk-shaped exit (1.5 stop, 3.0 target) loses $783. Widen the stop to 4×ATR and push the target to 6–8× and the same entries make +$3,484 to +$3,495 — a swing of over $4,200 on identical boards, purely on the exit.

But look at the surface, not the best cell. At a 4.0× stop, target 3.0 loses $1,792, target 4.0 loses $318, and target 6.0 makes $3,484. A real edge does not do that. That is not a plateau, it is a lottery, and it is the first sign that a handful of trades are carrying everything.

☠ The grave that matters most — a backtest bug that manufactured a winner

The chandelier family — wide stop, trail armed after N×ATR, give back k×ATR from the peak — came out of this sweep looking like the answer. It was the only exit family positive in nearly every regime cell across two different entry definitions, and chand_2.5_a1.5_t1.5 survived everything: +$4,270 net, +$4.58 a trade, 60% win, strip-best-3 still +$3,115, strip-best-10 still +$1,098, day-green 61%, worst day −$631.

It was wrong. The trail was testing the bar’s HIGH against the running peak instead of its LOW. A 5s bar that printed a new high and then collapsed registered a give-back of exactly zero, so the trail never fired inside the bar that actually broke it — it exited later, at prices that never traded. The peak was also read including the current bar, a second look-ahead on top of the first.

The tell was there before it was found, and it is worth naming so we catch the next one faster: the random-entry placebo. Firing at completely random minutes in a random direction with that same chandelier made +$1,542 on average, and the “real” rider’s +$4,270 sat at p = 0.115 against that null. A random entry should not make money. When your null hypothesis starts printing, the bug is in the machinery, not in the market.

The honest sweep — every cell judged, not just the best one

Cherry-picking the best cell out of a grid is how a desk convinces itself. So the full grid was run — 5 entry windows × 6 thresholds × 4 ER floors × 2 lockouts × 8 exits = 1,824 scored cells — and the same battery put on every one: net > 0, strip-best-3 > 0, strip-best-10 > 0, and both halves of the tape positive, with n ≥ 60.

If the edge were real and broad you would expect most of the 423 profitable cells to also be profitable in the back half; instead 423 shrinks to 36. The five “survivors” of the blanket sweep are exactly what chance hands you, and quoting any one of them would be a lie of selection.
