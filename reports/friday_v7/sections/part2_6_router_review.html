P2.6 THE ROUTER OPERATION REVIEW — week of 10–14 August 2026

Gaz, the router had a quiet, disciplined, slightly profitable week, and then I found two things underneath it that matter more than the week itself. The decisions it actually made were worth +$51.50 — its benches blocked $611 of losers and gave up $410 of winners (+$201), its armings paid $716.50 and cost $866 (−$149.50). That is a rounding error, and on a week where the tournament book closed −$325.00 over 108 trades a rounding error is not a bad result. The nightly rollup, which prices what the benched gates would have done, is more generous: +$400 net over the four days it can actually see.

The first thing underneath. The router is managing an empty room. Its managed universe is capitulation_long on the down side and rgv_short on the up side. Those two gates took 2 trades between them all week. Meanwhile exhaustion_short — which is an up-fader, exactly the shape the router exists to bench — took 71 of the week’s 108 trades, and 12 of them fired straight into a confirmed TREND_UP for −$241.00, at −$20.08 a trade. The nightly leakage detector reported zero leakage every single day, because it is only allowed to look at the two gates that never traded. That is not a small number and it is not a one-week fluke: over 30 days the same gate has 26 counter-trend fires for −$398.50, negative on 7 of the 8 days it happened, and it survives both strip-the-worst-day and leave-one-day-out. That is the Saturday play, and it is the best-evidenced thing in this section.

The second thing underneath. I do not fully trust the ruler the nightly uses, and I can prove it with the desk’s own trades. The nightly prices every blocked signal under a plain scalp-2R / 1-ATR exit. So I took the week’s 108 real live trades — same entries, same tape — and repriced them under that identical exit. They go from −$325.00 to +$459.50. The ruler is $7.26 a trade more generous than what the desk actually achieves, which means every “wins we missed” figure in the nightly is inflated. But — and this is why I segmented it before saying anything — the gap is almost exactly zero in chop (−$0.28 a trade over 65 trades) and huge in trend (+$25.02 in TREND_UP, +$11.38 in TREND_DOWN). The router benches in trend. So the over-benching it gets accused of is measured with a ruler that is at its most generous precisely where the router does its work.

Counting the switch changes — and why it has to be done three ways

The ledger learned this the hard way on Thursday morning and it is worth restating, because a churn metric read off the wrong instrument is worse than no churn metric. The router’s own log under-reports: its changed: field only records what the router changed, so the 22:00Z reactivation timer moves gates while every router tick that hour says “changed: none”. The systemd journal over-reports: tournament.py initialises its last-known set to empty, so the first tick after any restart logs a phantom change. Here is the reconciliation.

THE MODERATE RECOMMENDATION — and what I deliberately left out

TAKE: window 30→45 min, step 15→5 min, hold 2→3 marks. Three reasons, in order of weight. (a) It is the joint optimum on both universes independently — the live set and a set 2.7× larger with two different gates in it. (b) It never hurt a day on the live set and cost $10 on the worst day of the full set. (c) Most importantly, it is not a number found by searching — it is the mechanical fix for two problems this section documents from live evidence and not from the grid: a 45-minute window smooths the exact one-point boundary flicker that generated 36 flips on one gate, and 5-minute steps close the actuation lag that cost −$242 this week. When a grid search and a ledger written by hand arrive at the same answer from opposite directions, that is the strongest signal available here.

DO NOT TAKE: net 30→20. It is worth $13 on the full set and $31 on a live set of 14 trades. That is noise, it moves us to the edge of the plateau rather than the middle, and taking a free-looking knob change for no reason is how a config gets overfitted one dollar at a time. Leave ER 0.15 and net 30 exactly where they are.

The architecture question the week keeps asking

Two of the week’s three structural problems are not solvable by tuning the router, because they are not the router’s decisions. The 22:00Z reactivation timer made the two largest armings of the week (−$367.50 and +$310.00) and it is a clock, not a judgement — nothing grades it, and it re-arms gates the router benched hours earlier with no memory of why. The lapsed-bench unwind (−$232.00 this week) is the same defect in a different costume: a bench expiring is treated as an argument for arming. Both are the same bug — a permission that expires into the wrong default. The fix is one line of policy rather than any new mechanism: a bench that lapses returns a gate to BENCHED-BY-DEFAULT, and an arm needs a positive case made fresh. The router’s prompt already says this in words; the 22:00Z timer overrides it in code.
