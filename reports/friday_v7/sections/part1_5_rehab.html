P1.5 LIVE-DESK REHABILITATION — exhaustion_short, and the honest failures behind it

★ READ THIS FIRST — where this section came from

The dedicated rehabilitation phase of tonight’s run timed out at its 90-minute ceiling and never wrote its chapter (rehab: rc=-1 90.0m artifact=MISSING in data/friday_durable.log). It did, however, finish and save the entire battery it was told to run — 104 live exhaustion_short signals with the tick path after each one, four treatment grids, a 4,000-draw placebo behind every cell, two independent exit sweeps and a random-entry control. This section is assembled from those finished numbers, read out of the JSON at build time rather than retyped, so the figures are the study’s own. What is missing is the prose that phase would have written, and two rehabs it never got to. Those are named in the gap box at the end rather than quietly skipped.

One gate was properly red this week and it is the same one the scope flagged: exhaustion_short, which lost −$218.50 across 71 closed lots on the live desk and has now been red for long enough that “bench it” is the obvious call. The standing rule says we do not get to make the obvious call. So the study took every one of its 104 signals back to 24 July, rebuilt each one from the tape, and asked the four questions in order: are we counting malfunctions as strategy, is the true cost right, is the bleed in the BASE, the SIGNAL or the EXIT — and does any proposed fix survive a placebo. The answer is unusually clean, and it is not the answer anyone expected. Every treatment aimed at the ENTRY failed — cooldowns, benching after a run of stops, and a tighter flow floor all lose to random. The entry is not the problem. The exit is: the same signals, entered identically and held with a wide stop and a far target instead of scalped, are worth $3,459.98 against −$84.50 as the desk actually traded them, and the entries beat 60 of 60 random-entry controls under the same exit. The gate is rehabilitated as a SHADOW candidate, not deployed — and the reason it is not deployed is in §7.

1 · Normalise the malfunctions first — and this week there are none to strip

The first step of the wash is always to take the software failures out of the strategy verdict, because a gate that was never given a working stop has not been tested. Last week that step mattered enormously. This week it is a no-op, and that is itself the finding. Across the whole week the desk filled 62 of 62 stops, with no STOP_UNFILLED in 393 trades and 17 days — the resting-stop-on-a-concrete-future fix has held. So none of the numbers below need a malfunction adjustment, and every dollar exhaustion_short lost is a strategy dollar. Of its 104 signals, 52 ended at the stop — almost exactly half — and all of them were real fills at real prices.

2 · The corrected-cost reconstruct — what the gate really is

Priced honestly at $1.50 per round trip and $2.00 a point, over the full signal history rather than just this week, exhaustion_short is not a disaster. It is something more awkward: a coin-flip that pays slightly less than nothing.

3 · Root cause, attempt one: is it the ENTRY? Three treatments, all of them fail

The obvious diagnosis is that the gate keeps re-firing into a move that has already beaten it — it stops out, and then immediately shorts the same push again. That is a testable claim and it produces three concrete fixes, all three of which the operator has asked for by name at some point: cool off after a stop, bench after a run of stops, and demand more aggressor flow before firing at all. Every cell below is scored against 4,000 random draws that remove the same number of signals at random. That column is the one that matters: a filter that drops 30% of a losing book will usually look like it helped, and the placebo is what tells you whether it actually did anything a coin could not.

★ THE CONTRADICTION THE SCOPE ASKED US TO SETTLE

“Bench on a wall of stops” and “arm for periods” cannot both be right, and on this gate the wall-of-stops rule is simply wrong. Every version of it loses money here, and the reason is visible in the “signals cut” column: benching after two stops for an hour removes 18 winners to avoid 12 losers. The losing streak does not predict the next signal; it just tells you the last two went badly. Part 1 reached the same verdict from the live book — bench-on-2-stops would have cost the desk −$172 across this week and forfeited +$356 on Friday alone. Two independent populations, same answer. The discriminator is the REGIME the losses happened in, never the count of losses.

Three more checks, all pointing the same way. First, the correlation. Above the live floor, the relationship between how much flow a signal had and how much money it made is r = 0.059 on n = 70 (t = 0.49). That is nothing. More flow does not mean a better trade. Third, and this is what actually kills it: the era confound. The 17 signals that the “winning” floor removes are worth −$641.0 and contain 6 winners — but 12 of those 17 come from a single era. Honest instrument note. Of the 104 signals, 87 have usable tape; 17 fall on 2026-07-21, 2026-07-22, 2026-07-23, days the rolling capture window no longer holds, and those carry −$73.5. ★ REV2 — why this control says $3,465.48 and §7 says $3,459.98

They are the SAME policy and the gap is $5.50 across 87 signals — six cents a signal. Two things were making one result look like two. One, the units. This table labelled the target in R-multiples of the stop and §7 labels it in ATR; 2.0R of a 1.5×ATR stop is 3.0×ATR. Both now read in ATR. Two, the entry price. A paired control has to enter the real book the same way it enters its random books — at the TAPE price at the signal’s own timestamp — so that our execution quality cannot flatter the real leg; §7 enters at the live recorded fill. 8 · The verdict pills

Why this is SHADOW on Monday and not a deploy

Three reasons, and I would rather write them down than have them found later. (1) The sweep never turns over.
