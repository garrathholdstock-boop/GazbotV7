2.2   The wide profit leg beat the tight one by $331.50 — the week’s best-evidenced result

Every gate on this desk runs as a scale-out: one signal opens two one-lot sub-slots. Lot A takes a tight fixed target and banks certainty; Lot B runs wider or trails, and rides the tail. They share the same entry, the same price, and the same stop. 2.4   “Arm for periods” beat “bench on losses” — the contradiction, settled in money

There has been an unresolved argument sitting in the scope for two weeks: three stopped trades in a row is a textbook “wall of stop, bench it” trigger, but arming is supposed to be a permission window where early losses are the price of the option. Those two policies are directly opposed. This week gave us four clean instances, so I priced the rule instead of arguing about it.

The rule tested: after K consecutive signals where every lot stopped out, bench that gate for the rest of the day.

NET EFFECT OF THE BENCH-ON-2-STOPS RULE, WHOLE WEEK−$172.00

The rule costs money, and the reason is one specific session. On Friday exhaustion_short opened with two stops back to back — 13:38Z for −$87.50 and 13:42Z for −$107.00 — which is exactly the pattern that screams “bench it.” Bench it there and you forfeit +$356.00, because from 14:19Z onward that same gate went on to take twelve more signals and finish the day +$310.00. The two opening stops were the price of the option, precisely as the arm-for-periods argument says.

So what is the discriminator? It is the tape the losses happen in, not the losses. On Friday the two opening stops happened right before the 14:00Z hour did −108pt at ER 0.22 — a genuine down-leg that a short gate is built for. On Wednesday the equivalent pair happened inside an hour doing −33pt at ER 0.05, which is nothing at all, and there the bench would have saved $103. The instrument to bench on is the regime, not the scoreboard. At K=3 the rule fires only once all week and saves $71.50 — but that is a single instance and I would not build policy on it.

ON THE WIN RATE — the standing rule, honoured

43% is not the problem and I am not treating it as one. Nothing in this section is judged on win percentage. Every verdict below rests on expectancy ($ per signal after the real $1.50 round trip) and on robustness — placebo, strip-the-best, leave-one-day-out, both-halves. Where I kill something, I name the test that killed it. For the record: the desk’s two best days this week ran at 75% and 53%, and its worst ran at 25% — but Monday lost money at 30% while Thursday made $768.50 at 40%. The hit rate is not carrying the information.

5.3   And that morning, the switch file flipped seven times for nothing

Before any of the above, between 08:25Z and 12:35Z, the router flipped abs_veto_short on and off seven times — five of those inside fifty minutes. It produced zero trades and zero dollars. Two adjacent readings show why:

An arm/bench round trip decided by three points of a sixty-minute net — about one point of MNQ noise every twenty minutes. The direction state has a two-tick confirmation, but the ±40pt threshold underneath it has no hysteresis and no dwell at all, so a tape grinding either side of 40 manufactures churn that is perfectly correct by the rule and pure noise on the tape. It was free on Wednesday morning only because the gate happened to be silent. That afternoon, the 13:40Z re-arm off the same mechanism led directly to the 14:33Z fire that stopped for −$111.50. A dwell-time requirement on that threshold is the clearest single process fix in this section.

9 · TWO IDEAS THAT LOOKED GREAT AND DIED — with the test that killed each

Both of these are things I genuinely expected to survive. Neither did, and the way they failed is more useful than if they had worked.

9.2   A break-even stop — PARKED, killed by applying it to the winners too

Section 4 showed $1,058 of losses on trades that were up a median 39 points. The obvious fix is to move the stop to break-even once a trade is a certain distance onside. I replayed it on the 5-second bars, requiring the favourable move to happen in a bar strictly earlier than the exit bar so there is no cheating inside a bar.

Break-even armed atLosing lots rescued“Saving”Honest result — rule applied to every lot

That middle column is a lie and the right-hand column is the truth. Count only the losers a break-even stop rescues and it looks like $826 of free money. Apply the same rule to every lot — including the winners that dipped back through their entry price before running — and it costs between $304 and $490 at every threshold that matters. The one positive cell, +20 points for +$103, is non-monotonic against its neighbours, which is what noise looks like.

Verdict: PARKED, cause of death the apply-to-all-lots test. What would revive it: a version that arms on something other than a bare price touch — a dwell requirement (price holds beyond +X for N seconds), or arming only when the 30-minute efficiency clears a floor so it fires on moves that are actually going somewhere. The give-back problem it was aimed at is real and worth $1,058; this particular instrument just is not the answer, and the proper hunt for one belongs in the rehab section.

10 · WHAT I AM NOT SURE ABOUT

Standing habit: the caveats go in the section, not in a footnote nobody reads.

  • Everything here is one week and it is in-sample. 58 signals, 108 lots, five sessions. The A-versus-B result is the only thing with enough paired structure that I would defend it hard; everything else is an observation awaiting a second week.
  • Per-gate samples are small to the point of being decorative. abs_veto_long is five signals, capitulation_long is one. I have given them cards for completeness and graded neither. The day rider is five trades — a lovely five, and still five.
  • Two of the rider’s five trades are reconstructed, not observed, and four of five have no venue execution record. $263.50 of the $1,617.50 is our best backfill of what happened.
  • The break-even and wall-of-stop counterfactuals are replays, not trades. They assume our fills would have been where the bars say. The break-even one in particular does not model queue position at the entry price, which on a fast reversal is exactly where you would not get filled — so if anything it flatters the rule I am already rejecting.
  • The signal journal cannot tell us what the benches saved. All 222 rows have a NULL block reason, so “what would the benched gate have done” is not answerable from that table and I have not pretended otherwise anywhere in this section.
  • The 15-minute window on the MFE work is a choice. Most of these trades close inside five minutes, so it is generous to the ceiling and therefore harsh on our capture percentages. A shorter window would make our capture look better; I picked the one that flatters us least.

THE ONE STONE STILL UNTURNED

We now know where the money goes — $1,058 of it into trades that were up 39 points and gave it all back. We know the naive fix for that (a break-even stop) is a mirage. What we have not tested is a partial: banking one lot of the two at a fixed points-onside and letting the other run its existing exit. The scale-out already gives us two lots for free, so it costs nothing structurally, and unlike a break-even stop it does not require being right about where the pullback ends. That is the first thing I would test next week, and it is the natural bridge from this section into the rehab work.
